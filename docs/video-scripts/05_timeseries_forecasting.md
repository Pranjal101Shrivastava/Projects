## [0:00] Cold open

> **On screen —** the winners-by-series table

Most forecasting write-ups pick one time series, try a few models, and announce a winner.

The problem is that the answer depends almost entirely on which series you picked. So in
this project I ran 6 forecasters across 4 structurally different
real series — and on one of them, nothing I tried beat the stupidest possible baseline.

That's the result I want to spend the most time on.

## [0:29] The four series, and why these four

> **On screen —** the four series charts

These aren't four samples of the same thing. They're four different *shapes* of problem.

airline passengers, daily min temps, drug sales, sunspots.

One has a clean multiplicative trend with strong yearly seasonality. One is temperature —
seasonal, but noisy and mean-reverting. One is pharmaceutical sales, with both trend and
seasonality. And one is sunspots, which is where this gets interesting.

If you only test on the first one, you'll conclude that classical seasonal models win, and
you'll be right about that series and wrong about the general case.

## [1:08] The evaluation protocol

> **On screen —** the protocol description

Every model is evaluated the same way: rolling origin.

Rolling origin: 8 refits, each forecasting 12 steps ahead from data strictly before the origin. No model sees any observation at or after its origin. Reported MASE is the mean across origins, so a single lucky cut cannot determine the ranking.

That last sentence is the point. A single train-test cut is one sample of the model's
performance, and the variance between cuts is large enough to reverse a ranking. Eight
origins means a single lucky split can't decide the winner.

And the error measure is MASE — mean absolute scaled error. It's scaled by the naive
forecast's error on the training data, which means a MASE below one beats naive and above
one loses to it. The comparison is built into the metric.

## [2:07] The result

> **On screen —** the cross-series ranking table

Across all four series, SARIMA(1,1,1)(1,1,1,m) ranks first with a mean rank of
1.5, winning 3 of 4.

Which is roughly the expected answer. Classical seasonal models are strong on strongly
seasonal data, and gradient boosting — which people reach for by default now — came second
with 0 outright wins.

But the interesting row isn't at the top.

## [2:35] The series where nothing worked

> **On screen —** the sunspots chart and its MASE table

On sunspots, the winner is **seasonal naive**. The baseline. Repeat what
happened one cycle ago.

Every learned model lost to it.

And there's a reason, which is what makes it worth publishing rather than hiding. The
sunspot cycle averages about eleven years — but it isn't fixed, and it isn't anchored to
the calendar. Cycles run long, cycles run short, amplitudes vary enormously.

A seasonal model needs a fixed period to work with. Give it a period that drifts, and the
seasonal terms fit the average cycle and then gradually fall out of phase with the actual
one. The naive forecast has no period to get wrong.

So 1 of 4 series where the honest conclusion is: don't use a
model here, use the baseline.

If I'd run this study on one series, I'd have written "SARIMA wins" and I'd have been
wrong about the general case. Four series is still a small sample — I want to be clear
about that too — but it's enough to show the conclusion depends on the shape of the data.
